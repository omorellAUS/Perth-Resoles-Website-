Shoe Care Guide

Your shoes are your most important piece of gear. Here's how to look after them.

A good resole restores sticky grip, sharp edges and that perfect custom fit for a fraction of new shoe prices ($80–$130 vs $150-$200+). It can give your favourite pair another 3–4 lives while cutting down on waste.

But timing is everything. Resole your shoes too early and you waste rubber. Wait too long and you risk rand holes, upper damage,losing shape, extra repairs or even losing the shoe entirely. This guide gives you enough info to make the best choice for you every time.

Preventing excessive Toe wear through the rand:
When climbing regularly it’s easy to wear through your shoes fast, and when the front point of the shoe is excessively worn through, the rand then starts to wear through with it.

1. Quick Shoe Anatomy – The Parts That Matter for Resoling

Climbing shoes are hand-built around a plastic last (the mould that creates the aggressive shape, downturn and asymmetry). Here’s what wears:

  • Outsole – Thick sticky rubber on the bottom (4–5mm new). 90% of wear happens under the big toe.
  • Rand – Thin rubber band wrapped around the toe/heel. It protects the upper and holds tension for precision.
  • Midsole – Internal stiffener (foam/board) that gives support – stays in place during normal resoles.
  • Upper – Leather or synthetic body (Try to never let this get holes!).

The outsole and rand are glued on. Resoling replaces exactly these worn layers while keeping the shoe’s original shape and performance.

2. The Ideal Time to Resole – The Ideal Window

Send them when: The toe outsole is paper-thin (1mm or less) and shiny, but the rand is still fully protected. The clean seam between sole and rand starts to fade or dip inward (“seam dip” or “smiley face”).

Quick checks (do this every 10–15 sessions):

  • • Big toe area looks glossy and smooth
  • • Rubber feels mushy when you press with your thumb
  • • Edges are rounding off instead of crisp

Act now = perfect resole, no extra cost, performance back like new. Most experts agree, the best time to Resole is before you start climbing on the rand and wearing through the rand rubber.

3. When It’s Too Late – Avoid These Damage Signs

  • • Rand rubber showing or small holes
  • • Fabric/upper colour visible through sole
  • • Peeling, big holes or delamination
  • • Seam Dip – where the sole is almost worn through and starting to peel from the shoe rand
  • • Mushy Feel when applying thumb pressure to the shoe rand

• At this stage you’ll likely need an extra rand or toe-patch repairs (which adds time & cost). If damage goes right through the upper fabric, the shoe can be unrepairable. Climbing on the rand destroys tension and shape.

Pro tip: Most pairs can handle 3–4 quality resoles if you catch them early, or a rand repair can extend your shoes for multiple resoles.

5. Things to Avoid - Protect your shoes from shoe rot heat and damage

Preventing Heat Damage

  • Never leave shoes in a hot car: (especially exposed in the boot on a Perth summer day). Temperatures over ~60°C can start to melt glue, delaminate the rand/sole, deform the shape and ruin tension. Glue bonds loosen with heat, always place your shoes inside a bag and insulate best you can from the heat.
  • Avoid Prolonged direct sunlight or heat sources: when drying. UV and high heat shrink, harden leather/synthetics and weaken adhesives.
  • Other no-go’s: Walking excessively on concrete or outdoor trails in your climbing shoes, machine washing (warps shape), talcum powder (can migrate and weaken glue bonds), freezing or heating them in the oven damages the rubber & adhesives.

    Prevent shoe rot (mildew & bacteria):

  • Never stuff sweaty shoes straight into a sealed plastic bag or backpack after climbing.
  • Don’t leave them damp in a confined space + moisture + warmth = bacteria/mould and odour.
  • Best practice is Wipe the insoles and linings with a damp cloth after a weekend climbing and air dry completely in a cool, shaded spot.
  • When cleaning your shoes, Wash your shoes with warm soapy water and use a brush to exfoliate all built up grime and germs.

    7. Rubber Performance - Hardness, Resilience, and Abrasion Resistance

    Key Properties

    Shore Hardness (A Scale)
    Measures how hard or soft the rubber is.

    Higher number = firmer rubber.

    DIN Abrasion Resistance
    How well the rubber resists wearing down.

    Higher = more durable.

    Resilience / Rebound
    How “bouncy” and sticky the rubber is.

    Higher = better grip on smearing.
